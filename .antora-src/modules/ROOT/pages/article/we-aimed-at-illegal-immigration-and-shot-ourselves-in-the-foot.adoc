= We Aimed at Illegal Immigration and Shot Ourselves in the Foot

:page-author: Oleksandr Tishchenko
:description: A conservative systems-audit argument about how immigration fees, USCIS delays, benefit exclusions, housing instability, benefit-duration design, nonprofit capacity, and enforcement costs can jointly manufacture illegality inside the country.
:page-tags: migration policy, law and order, humanitarian status, USCIS fees, public benefits, lawful compliance, taxpayer cost, bad governance

Spoiler: this is not an article about open borders, and it is not an argument for unlimited benefits.
It is about a more dangerous systems problem: what happens when a government already allows or requires people to remain inside humanitarian or protection procedures, forbids work without authorization, delays or prices that authorization out of reach, removes food and housing bridges, refers families to nonprofits with no real capacity, and later spends taxpayer money enforcing against violations that this structure made predictable.

This is not only a SNAP issue, not only a USCIS fee issue, and not only one family's hardship.
It is an intersection problem: immigration status, work-authorization delays, U.S.-priced filing fees, fee waivers, public-benefit exclusions, benefit certification periods, unstable housing, nonprofit capacity, school disruption, and enforcement economics.

The core question is simple: if America wants less illegal work, less fraud, less homelessness, fewer missed notices, and lower enforcement costs, did the system leave people a realistic lawful path to compliance?
If the lawful path becomes too slow, too expensive, and practically unreachable, the state may not be preventing illegality.
It may be manufacturing it inside the country.

America has every right to control immigration.
A sovereign country is not required to open its doors to everyone who wants to enter.
The government has the right to verify status, restrict access to public programs, require documentation, fight fraud, stop unauthorized work, and protect taxpayer money.

But if the goal is law and order, the system must leave people a realistic way to obey the law.

The problem today is not simply that the rules are strict.
The problem is that they are increasingly strict in the wrong places.

When the government forbids a person to work without authorization but delays the authorization itself; when it charges hundreds of dollars for the very document required for lawful work; when fee waivers turn poverty into another bureaucratic exam; when basic survival bridges are removed; when the nonprofit safety net has no real capacity; and when enforcement later punishes people for violations that this structure predictably helped create, that is not law and order.

That is bad state engineering.

A policy may look tough on page one.
Toughness, by itself, is not effectiveness.
Sometimes a system does not prevent illegality; it manufactures it inside the country through delays, fees, procedural traps, and the absence of a lawful survival pathway.

Then the taxpayer pays twice: first for the bureaucracy that failed to let a person work lawfully on time, and then again for enforcement, courts, detention, emergency rooms, shelters, school disruption, and the social consequences of that same design failure.

== Strict policy must be intelligent policy

A strong state is not defined by closing every exit.
A strong state is defined by its ability to channel human behavior toward outcomes that benefit the country.

If the government wants less unauthorized work, lawful work must be fast, understandable, and practically reachable.
If the government wants less fraud, it must reduce incentives to survive through shadow arrangements.
If the government wants to protect taxpayer money, it must count not only benefit spending, but also the downstream cost of enforcement, homelessness, emergency medicine, school instability, and family collapse.

Bad policy often looks tough on the first page.
On the second page it becomes expensive.
On the third page it creates violations.
On the fourth page it demands more police, more courts, more detention, more bureaucracy, and more public spending.

That is not conservative efficiency.
That is expensive disorder.

== A migrant does not arrive from the American economy

A humanitarian migrant comes to the United States precisely because the United States is safer, wealthier, and institutionally stronger than the country they left.

That person does not arrive with an American wage.
They do not arrive with American savings.
They often arrive after war, persecution, destroyed housing, lost business, lost documents, family separation, or direct threats to safety.

For that reason, a government fee of $400, $500, or $560 is not a neutral amount.
Inside the United States, it may look like an unpleasant but familiar administrative charge.
For a person from a weaker economy, it may equal a full monthly wage.
For a family with children, it multiplies across every person and every renewal cycle.

USCIS announced that, effective January 1, 2026, certain immigration-related fees under H.R. 1 / OBBB increase; for example, an initial asylum-applicant EAD, an initial parole EAD, and an initial TPS EAD are listed at $560.footnote:[USCIS, _USCIS Announces FY 2026 Inflation Increase for Certain Immigration-Related Fees_, https://content.govdelivery.com/accounts/USDHSCIS/bulletins/3fc4a19]
For a family of five, that one type of document can mathematically become about $2,800 before postage, copies, transportation, legal help, lost work time, or re-filing costs.

In that context, an immigration fee stops being merely the cost of processing a form.
It becomes a test: can this person buy access to legal compliance before they are allowed to earn money in the economy where the fee was priced?

== Fees of this size are not pocket change even in America

The United States has large fees elsewhere.
For example, access to federal civil court itself can cost hundreds of dollars: the statutory filing fee for a federal district court is $350, not counting some additional administrative fees.footnote:[28 U.S.C. § 1914, https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title28-section1914]
That comparison shows that amounts in the $350-$560 range are serious legal-administrative entry barriers, not trivial household charges.

But immigration-document fees for humanitarian families can be recurring, family-wide, connected to survival, and connected to the ability to work lawfully.
If $560 is the price of a document without which a person cannot lawfully work, and that price is charged before the person reliably enters the lawful labor market, the fee is no longer merely cost recovery.
It becomes a gatekeeping device.

Even for low-income American households, $400-$560 is not a casual disposable amount.
The Bureau of Labor Statistics reported that in 2024 average annual expenditures for the lowest income quintile of consumer units were $35,046.footnote:[U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, _Consumer Expenditures -- 2024_, https://www.bls.gov/news.release/cesan.htm]
For a family without stable housing, without a first paycheck, with children, and without guaranteed work authorization, that amount is even less like a routine administrative expense.

== A fee waiver does not remove the barrier; it bureaucratizes poverty

The standard answer is: there is a fee waiver.
But a fee waiver is not the absence of a barrier.
It is another door, with another form, another evidentiary burden, another wait, and another risk of rejection.

The applicant must know the waiver exists, understand the instructions, gather proof of poverty, unstable housing, lack of income, medical expenses, or family burden, submit everything correctly, wait for review, and hope the request is not denied or returned.
USCIS describes fee waiver requests as a separate request filed with the underlying form where the applicant is otherwise eligible.footnote:[USCIS, _Fee Schedule_, https://www.uscis.gov/g-1055]

If a person cannot pay for a work permit because they have no job, and the work-permit process is delayed because they must prove they have no money, the system closes the loop.
The fee waiver does not eliminate the poverty barrier.
It bureaucratizes poverty.

== A work permit is not an instant paycheck

Even when a person is theoretically eligible for employment authorization, that does not mean they will have a job, wages, and stable housing tomorrow.

For asylum applicants, timing itself is a major problem.
USCIS describes the 180-Day Asylum EAD Clock: in general terms, an applicant is not eligible for an asylum-based EAD until the asylum application has been pending for enough time.footnote:[USCIS, _Applicant-Caused Delays in Adjudications of Asylum Applications and Impact on Employment Authorization_, https://www.uscis.gov/sites/default/files/document/notices/Applicant-Caused-Delays-in-Adjudications-of-Asylum-Applications-and-Impact-on-Employment-Authorization.pdf]

But even after an EAD is approved, the chain is not over.
It has only begun:

status -> EAD filing -> fee or fee waiver -> USCIS wait -> card delivery -> job search -> interview -> onboarding -> first paycheck.

Each step can take weeks or months.
A government document that may be reviewed someday does not pay for tonight's hotel room.

== A document can arrive after its useful life has already been consumed

In TPS and similar categories, the problem is compounded by limited document duration.
If the government takes too long to process a document, or the document arrives close to expiration, it becomes a strange object: formally an authorization, but practically a late piece of paper.

A person paid the fee or went through the waiver, waited, complied with the rules, and then received not a practical tool for work, but a record that the system once recognized eligibility after much of the useful window had already passed.

If the government charges for a document but does not guarantee timely issuance, does not extend the practical term of usefulness when delay is agency-caused, and does not provide a bridge during the wait, the risk is shifted entirely onto the family.

== Public benefits were a compliance bridge, not free money

Public benefits should not be understood only as a gift or as easy money.
In many cases, they function as a compliance bridge inside the broader state machine.

When one part of the government delays work authorization, another part of the public system historically absorbs part of the survival pressure through food assistance, cash assistance, school support, medical support, or housing-related referrals.
That is not merely help to the applicant.
It is a mechanism that keeps people inside lawful behavior.

Without such a bridge, the system pressures people toward three bad outcomes:

. unauthorized work;
. homelessness and family breakdown;
. disappearance from the visible system, missed notices, lost procedures, and further sanctions.

But benefit design is not a binary switch between give everyone everything and cut everyone off.
The government has at least three risk-control levers: eligibility, amount, and duration.
If the state worries about dependency, fraud, or overly cheap access to cash or food support, it can reduce the amount or shorten the certification period: for example, use a three-month bridge instead of a longer cycle, with renewed review of income, housing, and work-authorization status.

This is especially logical where agencies already ask for recent income records, recent bank records, and proof from the last few months.
If the administrative system already evaluates a short recent period, short certification can operate as a control valve instead of categorical exclusion.

But that lever has a cost.
Every renewal, upload, phone call, notice, interview, proof request, and delay creates a transaction cost for a poor family.
The bureaucratic barrier becomes part of the price of the benefit: time, postage, transportation, phone access, translation, documents, stress, and the risk of a missed notice.
A shorter certification period can be a rational control tool only if the process is fast, clear, and actually accessible.
Otherwise the state may appear to save money on the benefit while creating a new cost of compliance.

After H.R. 1 / OBBB, federal SNAP eligibility for many noncitizens was narrowed while certain immigration-related filing fees were increased.footnote:[USDA Food and Nutrition Service, _One Big Beautiful Bill Act_, https://www.fns.usda.gov/obbb; USCIS, _USCIS Announces FY 2026 Inflation Increase for Certain Immigration-Related Fees_, https://content.govdelivery.com/accounts/USDHSCIS/bulletins/3fc4a19]
Even if such decisions are within Congress's power, the systems question remains: what happens to people whom the same government allows or requires to remain in humanitarian or protection procedures but leaves without a lawful survival bridge?

== Policy also sends a social signal

Law is not only an instruction to agencies.
Law is also a signal to society.

When the government says that certain families already inside a lawful or humanitarian process no longer have access to basic bridge programs, it sends a message to employers, landlords, churches, neighbors, donors, and local communities.
The message is: these people are outside ordinary public solidarity.

A private person may think: if the government itself cut them off, helping them must be risky, pointless, politically toxic, or simply not my problem.
In that way, a public exclusion rule can also close informal solidarity: private charity, fundraising, church support, landlord flexibility, and employer goodwill.

== The system can create the violations it later punishes

If a person cannot work without an EAD, but the EAD is delayed; if the EAD costs hundreds of dollars, but the fee waiver delays or complicates the process; if benefits are closed; if housing help is practically unavailable; if charities have sympathy but no capacity; and if notices arrive through fragile channels, then violation becomes not an ideological choice but a predictable outcome of design.

The government does not need to order illegality in order to manufacture illegal pressure.
It only needs to close the lawful exits.

Then the same system may later use the resulting violation as a basis for sanctions, denial, enforcement interest, admissibility problems, a bad moral character narrative, or future immigration barriers.

== Taxpayers pay for bad design

If cutting a food bridge pushes families into homelessness, emergency rooms, informal labor, school instability, shelter systems, court systems, and enforcement pipelines, the taxpayer may not save money.
The taxpayer may simply pay through a different department.

Budget discipline is not the same thing as moving costs from one ledger to another.

If the goal is fewer violations and lower public cost, then compliance must be cheaper than noncompliance.
Lawful work must be easier than cash labor.
Timely documentation must be easier than disappearance.
Communication with agencies must be easier than losing the case because the notice went to the wrong address.

== Smarter strictness: make compliance fast, cheap, and useful

A smarter conservative policy would not need to choose between open borders and bureaucratic cruelty.
It could be stricter and more functional at the same time.

For example:

* low-cost or no-cost humanitarian EADs;
* automatic fee relief for families without income;
* expedited EAD processing for households with minor children;
* automatic extension of useful document validity when delay is agency-caused;
* temporary food bridge until EAD approval or first paycheck;
* shorter benefit certification periods when the government wants more frequent review instead of full exclusion;
* child-centered subsistence protection;
* reliable notice systems for unstable housing;
* no punitive consequence where the violation was foreseeably caused by government delay and lack of a lawful survival path.

This is not open borders.
This is not free benefits for everyone.
This is basic state engineering.

If the government demands compliance, it must preserve a realistic way to comply.

== Conclusion

This problem is not only about benefits.
It is not only about immigration fees.
It is not only about USCIS delays.
It is not only about one state, one agency, or one caseworker.

The problem is cumulative.

The system recognizes humanitarian and protection-seeking pathways; forbids unauthorized work; delays or complicates access to work authorization; charges U.S.-priced fees before the applicant has reliable access to the U.S. labor market; turns fee waivers into a separate bureaucratic process; narrows subsistence benefits instead of using narrower tools such as amount limits or shorter certification periods; refers families to nonprofits that often have no housing or cash capacity; and then may punish people for the violations this structure predictably pressured them to commit.

That is not merely a policy gap.
It is an architecture of forced vulnerability.

Reasonable people can debate what benefits should exist, who should receive them, and for how long.
But if the government creates or recognizes a humanitarian status, accepts fees for documents, forbids work without authorization, delays authorization, and removes the basic bridge to survival, it must also accept responsibility for the predictable result.

If the goal is law and order, lawful compliance must be practically possible.
Otherwise we aimed at illegality and shot ourselves in the foot.
