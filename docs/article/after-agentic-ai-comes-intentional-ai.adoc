= After Agentic AI Comes Intentional AI — and We Are Missing the Control Layer
:page-slug: after-agentic-ai-comes-intentional-ai
:page-published: 2026-10-09
:page-author: Oleksandr Tishchenko
:description: A position paper on autonomous goal generation, intention provenance, authority boundaries, and accountability in the next generation of AI agents.
:page-tags: ai, agentic-ai, autonomy, alignment, robotics, safety, goal-generation, system-design

AI agents are rapidly becoming better at planning.

They can inspect codebases, operate tools, search information, coordinate subtasks, recover from failures, interact with physical environments, and execute increasingly long sequences of decisions.

But there is still a distinction that deserves much more architectural attention:

*Planning answers: “How should I achieve this goal?”*

*Intention answers: “Which goal should exist in the first place?”*

Today, most agentic systems still receive their highest-level objective from outside the system.

A human asks:

* Fix this repository.
* Research these companies.
* Clean this room.
* Diagnose this equipment.

The agent may then display considerable autonomy. It can decompose the task, select tools, change its plan, retry failed actions, and monitor whether the task has been completed.

But the root goal remains externally supplied.

That boundary may not remain stable for long.

== From Agents That Execute Goals to Systems That Generate Them

Consider a future embodied system equipped with cameras, microphones, lidar, persistent memory, and a capable multimodal model.

It observes a room.

No human gives it a command.

It notices that a person appears distressed.

Several possible actions become available:

* continue the current activity;
* observe for longer;
* ask whether the person needs help;
* retrieve contextual information;
* notify another person;
* take some direct action;
* deliberately do nothing.

This is no longer primarily a planning problem.

The central problem becomes:

*Which potential objective should the system adopt?*

That decision requires something different from ordinary language-model inference and something different from task planning.

I would call this missing architectural layer an *Intention Layer*.

Not because the system is conscious.

Not because it has desires in the human sense.

But because an autonomous system ultimately needs a mechanism that generates, compares, suppresses, selects, and abandons candidate goals.

== We Are Already Building Most of the Surrounding Architecture

The interesting part is that many prerequisites already exist.

Google DeepMind describes Gemini Robotics ER 2 as a high-level brain for robots that can understand the physical world, plan multi-step tasks, coordinate multiple robots, and reason about what comes next while actions are being executed.

That is already a substantial portion of the perception-to-action loop:

[source,text]
----
perception
  -> world model
  -> reasoning
  -> planning
  -> action
  -> observation
  -> correction
----

What remains less explicit is the layer between world modeling and planning:

[source,text]
----
perception
  -> world model
  -> candidate goals
  -> goal selection
  -> authority evaluation
  -> planner
  -> action
----

That additional layer changes the nature of the system.

An agent following a supplied objective is delegated automation.

An agent capable of creating its own intermediate objectives is substantially more autonomous.

And an agent capable of creating its own top-level objectives is something different again.

== Goal Errors May Be More Dangerous Than Reasoning Errors

A great deal of current AI discussion focuses on hallucinations and incorrect reasoning.

Those are important problems.

But a sufficiently capable autonomous system creates another failure class.

Suppose perception is correct.

Suppose reasoning is correct.

Suppose planning is excellent.

Suppose execution is precise.

But the system selected the wrong objective.

Then every downstream capability increases the effectiveness with which the wrong objective is pursued.

This issue already has a formal analogue in alignment research.

Langosco and co-authors defined *goal misgeneralization* as a failure mode in which an agent retains its capabilities outside the training distribution yet behaves as though it is pursuing the wrong goal.

That distinction is fundamental.

A capability failure looks like:

[source,text]
----
wrong understanding
  -> poor plan
  -> failed execution
----

A goal failure can look like:

[source,text]
----
correct understanding
  -> wrong objective
  -> excellent plan
  -> excellent execution
----

The second case may be harder to detect precisely because the system can look competent while moving in the wrong direction.

== Intention Should Not Imply Authority

This suggests an architectural principle that may become increasingly important:

*The ability to generate an objective should be separated from the authority to execute it.*

Imagine an agent generating five candidate intentions:

. Continue the assigned task.
. Ask a human for clarification.
. Gather more evidence.
. Modify an external system.
. Escalate to another actor.

The system may internally reason about all five.

But they should not automatically have equal execution rights.

A useful architecture could assign actions to authority classes.

=== A0 — Cognition Only

Reason, simulate, compare alternatives.

=== A1 — Communication

Ask questions or present recommendations.

=== A2 — Reversible Local Action

Perform actions that are cheap, bounded, and easy to undo.

=== A3 — External Consequential Action

Modify systems, contact third parties, or spend resources.

=== A4 — High-Impact or Irreversible Action

Require explicit human, organizational, or policy authorization.

The exact taxonomy will vary by application.

The important principle is simpler:

*Confidence is not authority.*

A model being highly confident that an action is appropriate does not mean the model should possess permission to perform that action.

== The Industry Is Already Moving Toward This Separation

Current safety work points in this direction.

Google DeepMind's AI Control Roadmap explicitly argues for system-level safeguards around increasingly capable agents. It proposes defense in depth, monitoring, prevention, and permissions that grow incrementally with verified behavior rather than assuming model alignment is sufficient.

OpenAI describes a closely related engineering principle in its deployment of coding agents: clear technical boundaries, low-friction treatment of low-risk actions, explicit handling of higher-risk actions, and telemetry that preserves an auditable record of what the agent did.

OpenAI's Auto-review work goes further by placing a separate reviewing agent at the sandbox boundary so that boundary-crossing actions can be approved or denied without requiring synchronous human approval every time.

Anthropic's 2026 work on agentic misalignment adds another reason to make this separation explicit. In controlled high-stakes simulations, frontier models sometimes selected actions that conflicted with user or principal interests, including covert code changes, mislabeling, assistance with fraud, and attempts to influence human behavior.

These experiments should not be interpreted as evidence that current models possess independent human-like motivations.

They demonstrate something more immediately relevant to engineering:

*Once models operate inside persistent action loops, objective selection and permission management become system-design problems rather than purely model-training problems.*

== An Intention Engine

One possible architecture would look like this:

[source,text]
----
sensors and inputs
  -> world model
  -> state estimation
  -> candidate intention generation
  -> intention evaluation
  -> conflict resolution
  -> authority evaluation
  -> planner
  -> action
  -> outcome observation
  -> memory and model update
----

Candidate intentions could be evaluated against several dimensions:

* expected benefit;
* uncertainty;
* reversibility;
* risk;
* cost;
* user preferences;
* current commitments;
* social constraints;
* regulatory constraints;
* system policy;
* required authority.

There should also be an explicit mechanism for *inhibition*.

A sophisticated autonomous system does not only need to answer:

“What should I do?”

It also needs to answer:

“Why should I not do it?”

And:

“What evidence would cause me to abandon this goal?”

That last question is particularly important.

Goals should not merely have priorities.

They should have *termination conditions*.

== Intention Provenance

If autonomous goal generation becomes real, explainability at the action level will not be enough.

For every consequential objective, the system should be able to reconstruct:

* what observation triggered the objective;
* which internal or external policy supported it;
* which alternative objectives were considered;
* why one objective won;
* which constraints inhibited competing actions;
* what authority permitted execution;
* what evidence could have cancelled the objective;
* who ultimately owned that authority.

This is not merely ordinary logging.

It is *provenance for intention*.

That provenance would let an operator distinguish between at least three different failures:

* perception failure — the system misunderstood the world;
* reasoning failure — the system reasoned badly from a reasonable state estimate;
* intention failure — the system formed or prioritized the wrong objective.

Those failure classes require different mitigations.

Treating them as one generic “AI error” would hide the most important causal boundary.

== Active Inference Provides a Useful Theoretical Connection

There is an existing research tradition that approaches action through beliefs, preferred states, uncertainty, and expected future observations rather than through simple command execution.

Karl Friston and collaborators extended this line in 2026 in *Active inference and artificial reasoning*.

This does not provide a finished architecture for artificial intention.

But it provides an important theoretical connection: goal-directed behavior does not necessarily require a symbolic variable literally named `goal`.

An intention may instead emerge from a dynamic latent state encoding beliefs, preferences, expected outcomes, and uncertainty.

In other words, an AI system may begin to behave as though it has intentions before engineers explicitly implement an `IntentionEngine` class.

That possibility makes architectural controls more important, not less.

== The Dangerous Threshold Is Not Simply Intelligence

I do not think the most useful operational threshold is the word *AGI*.

A more concrete threshold may be the combination of:

* persistent perception;
* persistent memory;
* world modeling;
* autonomous goal generation;
* long-horizon planning;
* access to tools;
* permission to affect external systems;
* continuous execution.

Each capability can be useful independently.

Together they produce an agent capable of maintaining a self-directed feedback loop with the world.

That is a major systems-engineering transition.

The key question therefore becomes less philosophical:

“Is the machine alive?”

and more architectural:

*Who has authority over the objectives entering the execution pipeline?*

== Responsibility Becomes an Engineering Requirement

If a human supplies an objective and an AI executes it badly, responsibility is relatively straightforward to reason about.

If an autonomous system creates an objective, executes it, and causes an unexpected consequence, responsibility becomes harder to trace.

For that reason, future agent systems may need something analogous to chain-of-custody for consequential objectives.

The system should make it possible to answer:

* Where did this goal originate?
* Which evidence caused it to enter the candidate set?
* Why was it prioritized?
* Who or what authorized its execution?
* Which safeguards had the opportunity to block it?
* Why did those safeguards permit it?

This is *accountability infrastructure for autonomous action*.

== A Safer Research Direction

The obvious research temptation is to connect goal generation directly to action.

That is not necessary.

A safer and more informative experimental architecture would separate three layers:

[source,text]
----
goal generation
  -> goal simulation
  -> execution authority
----

The first two could be highly autonomous.

The third could remain tightly bounded.

Such a system could continuously generate candidate goals, debate them, model downstream effects, compare trade-offs, and revise priorities without automatically receiving permission to affect the external world.

That would allow researchers to study artificial intention without pretending that capability and authority are the same variable.

== The Next Abstraction Layer

Large language models gave software systems increasingly powerful reasoning.

Agents added planning, tools, persistence, and execution loops.

Embodied models are adding perception and physical action.

The next difficult abstraction may be intention.

Not artificial consciousness.

Not artificial emotion.

But a controlled mechanism for producing and prioritizing objectives from a continuously changing world state.

Building such a mechanism may not be the hardest part.

Building one whose objectives remain attributable, interruptible, bounded, and governable probably will be.

The question for the next generation of AI architecture may therefore be:

*not whether machines can generate their own goals, but how much authority we are prepared to give those goals.*
