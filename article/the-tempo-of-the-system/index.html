The Tempo of the System

Source asciidoc: `docs/article/the-tempo-of-the-system.adoc` © 2026 Oleksandr Tishchenko / Marketing America Corp LLC. Distribution is permitted only with reference to the author and the original source.

Abstract

Modern science measures time with extraordinary precision. But precision does not automatically answer a deeper question: what exactly are we measuring?

This essay develops a speculative hypothesis: metric time may be an excellent local metrology of processes inside the present regime of the Universe without necessarily being the fundamental measure of the full trajectory of the system itself.

The observer, memory, and clock are all parts of the same system. They can compare internal processes with exceptional consistency, but they possess no genuinely external standard from which to measure the absolute tempo of the whole.

The proposal is therefore not that "time used to run faster." That statement already assumes another time. The proposal is subtler: the tempo of the system itself may change as the system moves through different dynamical regimes.

1. Equal Ticks Are a Metrological Achievement, Not Yet an Ontology

Civilization requires repeatable units. Navigation, communication, experiments, and engineering need today’s second to match tomorrow’s second.

Atomic time is excellent precisely because its tick is stable and reproducible.

But from this success we often make a stronger inference:

If equal ticks describe the present system extremely well, then equivalent equal ticks must be the natural fundamental measure of the entire history of that system.

That inference is not itself a measurement. It is an assumption about the structure of reality.

A clock can be perfect for local duration and still be silent about whether local duration is the fundamental way to describe the evolving system that contains the clock.

2. The Observer Is Inside the System

The phrase "we observe the Universe" hides an abstraction.

The observer is made of the same system. The brain, telescope, atom, radioactive isotope, and theory are all internal.

Physically, the situation is closer to:

one process in the system -> interacts with another process in the system

We can compare internal ratios with extraordinary precision, but every term in the comparison belongs to the same reality.

Accuracy of internal relations therefore does not by itself establish an independent external rhythm.

3. An External Observer Cannot Carry the "True Clock"

Imagine an observer outside the system.

If it can distinguish A, then B, then C, it must itself possess distinguishable states. A living observer perceives and remembers. A machine changes its register.

So even the external observer becomes another process.

It does not need metric clocks, but it has a tempo of change against which another system may appear faster or slower.

Conceptually:

\$R_{AB}=\frac{\Gamma_A}{\Gamma_B}.\$

This suggests a radical possibility:

Perhaps the primitive concept is not time as a container, but relations between changing systems and their tempos.

4. Stop Saying "Time Used to Run Faster"

That phrase secretly introduces a second time.

Instead introduce an abstract parameter:

\$q.\$

For now, q is neither seconds nor literal velocity. It represents a dynamical regime of the system.

Let q near zero describe an early regime closer to an idealized static state, and q approaching a limit describe an increasingly developed dynamical regime.

Now define:

\$\Gamma(q),\$

the internal tempo of processes.

The central speculative hypothesis is:

\$\frac{d\Gamma}{dq}<0.\$

As the system moves toward a more developed dynamical regime, its internal processes become less intense relative to the trajectory of the system itself.

The claim is not that a second becomes longer. The claim is that the regime from which seconds emerge as a useful metric may itself change.

5. A Nonlinear Trajectory and the Long Slow Tail

Two broad versions are possible.

In a simple version, q progresses relatively smoothly while internal tempo gradually decreases.

In a nonlinear version, the progression of the regime itself changes. History may then contain an intense early region, transition regimes, and a very long late tail.

We may exist inside such a tail.

If so, today’s tempo feels fundamental because every human experience, laboratory, and clock exists inside that late regime.

6. The Speed of Light Is Only an Image of a Limit

It is tempting to write q = v/c and imagine a transition from zero toward the speed of light.

As intuition, this is useful. As physics, it is premature, because standard cosmology does not provide a velocity of the Universe relative to an external space.

For now q should remain an unknown dimensionless parameter. Only later could one ask whether it maps to expansion, density, entropy, causal connectivity, or another physical quantity.

7. The Clock Is One of the Breaths

An atomic clock does not stand beside the system and observe its breathing.

The clock is one of the breaths.

The same is true of radioactive decay, chemical reactions, metabolism, neural activity, orbital motion, and biological reproduction.

If a common factor changed many processes in exactly the same way, the internal observer might never detect it. Locally, one tick would still equal one tick.

The possible mistake begins only when stability of the local tick is promoted into proof that the entire historical trajectory possesses the same natural metric.

8. Amount of Change Is Not Time

A different shortcut also fails: perhaps the amount of structural change itself is the true clock.

Imagine a global catastrophe today that transforms half the planet.

The structural change may be enormous, yet the catastrophe remains today.

Therefore the hypothesis must distinguish:

  • magnitude of transformation,

  • tempo at which transformations occur,

  • dynamical regime in which the system performs them.

This prevents the idea from collapsing into a simple state-distance theory.

9. Dinosaurs "Behind Us"

To say that dinosaurs are "almost behind my back" is nonsense in ordinary chronology.

The phrase means something narrower here.

A huge accumulated number of internal late-regime ticks does not necessarily have to correspond to an equally huge displacement along a deeper dynamical trajectory.

Thus "66 million years" can remain a valid metric coordinate while being a poor intuition for the system’s position on another still-hypothetical trajectory.

This does not reject paleontological dating. It questions whether chronological depth and dynamical depth must be identical concepts.

10. Evolution Was the First Source of the Discomfort

Evolutionary biology already rejects a constant rate of change. Generation length, population structure, selection, drift, isolation, climate, and ecological opportunity all matter.

The present hypothesis does not replace those mechanisms.

It asks an additional question:

Do we also assume, without noticing it, that the baseline tempo of the larger physical-biological system throughout evolutionary history was equivalent to the tempo of the regime in which we measure it now?

11. Resistance to Change

Introduce a second conceptual quantity:

\$R(q),\$

the resistance of the system to substantial change.

This is not yet energy in joules, mass, or mechanical work.

Suppose:

\$\frac{dR}{dq}>0.\$

Then the conjecture becomes a coupled pair:

\$\Gamma(q)\downarrow,\qquad R(q)\uparrow.\$

An early compact regime could be highly transformable. A later, larger, more separated and structurally mature regime could make large coordinated changes increasingly difficult.

This is more precise than saying "the past required less energy." Standard cosmology describes the early Universe as extremely hot and energy-dense. The speculative quantity here is resistance to a class of structural transitions, not joules.

12. Human Longevity Is a Stress Test, Not Evidence

Human life expectancy has increased dramatically, but that fact alone does not support this hypothesis.

Lower infant mortality, sanitation, nutrition, vaccination, antibiotics, safer childbirth, cardiovascular medicine, and other cultural and medical factors explain much of the increase.

Moreover, maximum human longevity has not obviously accelerated toward several hundred years, and gains in the longest-lived populations have become harder to extend.

That is a direct challenge to a naive version of the idea.

The claim cannot be:

People live longer now, therefore the whole system is slowing down.

13. Biological Tempo of State Progression

Life expectancy, realized lifespan, and biological aging rate are different variables.

Two people with the same chronological age can differ substantially in physiological state.

An organism can be represented conceptually as moving through:

B0 -> B1 -> B2 -> ... -> Bsenescence

Chronological clocks measure how many metric years the path occupies.

They do not guarantee:

\$\frac{dB}{dt}=\mathrm{const}.\$

This suggests a useful term: biological tempo of state progression.

The fact that biological progression is not uniform relative to chronological time does not prove a cosmological change in system tempo. It establishes a more modest principle: uniform metric ticks can coexist with non-uniform internal progression of a real system.

14. Stress and Local Acceleration of Biological Wear

The phrase "stress less and live longer" is crude, but chronic stress is associated with pathways involved in accelerated biological aging.

The careful reformulation is not "move slowly and time slows."

It is:

Do not unnecessarily increase the internal rate of damage and physiological wear of the organism.

Psychological calm, bodily movement, metabolism, and biological aging are different quantities.

This motivates three conceptual levels:

\$\Gamma_U,\quad\Gamma_B,\quad\Gamma_I,\$

where the symbols represent a hypothetical system-wide baseline, a biological-system tempo, and the realized tempo of an individual organism.

Conceptually:

\$\Gamma_I=\Gamma_U\times F(\text{genetics, stress, nutrition, disease, environment, ...}).\$

Known biological factors may be vastly larger than any hypothetical system-wide effect.

15. How Biology Could Falsify the Hypothesis

A true system-wide effect should not exist only in humans.

After controlling for known causes, some coherent tendency would have to appear across independent systems: wild mammals, birds, reptiles, trees, marine organisms, microorganisms, cell lines, or developmental processes.

If no common historical signal exists after environmental and evolutionary corrections, that counts against a strong system-tempo hypothesis.

If a weak cross-lineage effect appeared that resisted ordinary explanations, it would become an anomaly worth investigating.

At present, such evidence has not been established.

16. The Main Physical Vulnerability: Universal Rescaling

Suppose every process changes by the same factor K(q):

\$\Gamma_A(q)=K(q)\Gamma_A^0,\$
\$\Gamma_B(q)=K(q)\Gamma_B^0.\$

Then:

\$\frac{\Gamma_A(q)}{\Gamma_B(q)} = \frac{\Gamma_A^0}{\Gamma_B^0}.\$

An internal observer cannot detect K(q).

At that point, saying "everything used to happen faster" may be only a reparameterization.

To become physics, the model needs at least one internally observable relation that varies with q.

That is why dimensionless ratios matter.

Potential domains include spectral ratios, fundamental dimensionless constants, nucleosynthesis, the cosmic microwave background, independent radioactive systems, stellar physics, geological clocks, and independent biological rates.

If no such distinction can exist even in principle, the proposal remains metaphysics.

17. Dating Systems Must Not Be Dismissed Cheaply

Radiocarbon dating requires calibration because atmospheric carbon-14 has varied. That is a useful reminder that a physical signal is connected to a historical coordinate through a model.

But calibration is based on independent records and cross-checks. Other geological intervals use other isotopic systems and physical constraints.

A serious alternative model must therefore explain why independent clocks agree as well as they do.

Cross-consistency is evidence to confront, not an inconvenience to remove.

18. Memory Is an Analogy, Not a Clock

Human memory does not store elapsed seconds. It organizes the past through sequence, causality, significance, and personal transformation.

An event twenty years old can feel immediately behind us, while one intense month can feel like another life.

This does not demonstrate cosmological nonlinearity.

It merely shows that metric time is not the only way a system can organize its own history.

19. Are We Discovering the Past or Completing a Story?

Science is performed by brains that evolved to search for patterns, causes, stable categories, and useful predictions.

The scientific method is powerful because it constrains those tendencies.

A useful hypothesis must collide with observation.

The important question is therefore not:

Is this a compelling story?

but:

What observation would force us to abandon it?

That standard applies especially strongly to the speculative framework proposed here.

20. Working Canon

To keep the idea from sliding into easier but inaccurate formulations:

  1. Metric time is not declared false.

  2. The claim is not that seconds used to be shorter.

  3. There is no privileged external observer carrying the true metric clock.

  4. The primitive object of interest is the relation between tempos of changing processes.

  5. A dynamical-regime parameter q is introduced, but its physical identity is unknown.

  6. The central conjecture is dGamma/dq < 0.

  7. A second conjecture is dR/dq > 0.

  8. Amount of change is not time.

  9. Increasing human lifespan is not evidence by itself.

  10. Biological tempo of state progression is a possible stress test, not a proof.

  11. A strong system-wide effect should not be unique to humans.

  12. Without a changing observable ratio, the hypothesis may reduce to a coordinate transformation.

Conclusion

One of humanity’s most productive habits is converting change into sequences of equal units.

That habit gave us clocks, navigation, experimental physics, chronology, and cosmology.

Its usefulness is beyond dispute.

But usefulness of a coordinate does not prove that the coordinate is a primitive constituent of reality.

We measure the Universe from inside it. Our clocks belong to it. Our brains belong to it. Any imagined observer capable of distinguishing succession becomes a process as soon as it observes.

It therefore remains logically possible that the deeper structure is not a stream of equal parcels of time, but changing systems and relations among their tempos.

In such a picture, an early system could occupy a regime of intense internal transformation and comparatively low resistance to some structural transitions. As its regime develops, internal tempo could decrease while resistance to major transformation increases. Late history would then form a long slow tail.

An observer emerging inside that tail would construct an excellent local metronome and could understandably mistake its local regularity for the universal ruler of history.

This is not yet a theory.

It is not yet a complete physical hypothesis.

Several observations constrain naive versions of it, and those objections should remain visible. Human longevity is not a simple confirmation. Independent dating systems must continue to agree. A universal rescaling that changes no dimensionless ratio may have no physical content.

The next stage cannot be another metaphor.

It must ask:

What observation cannot be explained by ordinary physics and biology, but follows naturally from a changing tempo of the containing system?

Until such an observation exists, this remains a philosophical-physical construction.

For now, the central question is simpler:

What if we are measuring history with excellent clocks, while the clocks themselves are part of the changing process whose tempo we are trying to understand?
