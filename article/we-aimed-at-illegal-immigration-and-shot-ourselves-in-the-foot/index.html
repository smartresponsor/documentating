We Aimed at Illegal Immigration and Shot Ourselves in the Foot

Why a system that delays work permits, raises fees, removes survival bridges, and then punishes predictable violations is not law and order — it is bad governance.

America has every right to control immigration. A sovereign country is not required to open its doors to everyone who wants to enter. The government has the right to verify status, restrict access to public programs, require documentation, fight fraud, stop unauthorized work, and protect taxpayer money.

But if the goal is law and order, the system must leave people a realistic way to obey the law.

The problem today is not simply that the rules are strict. The problem is that they are increasingly strict in the wrong places.

When the government forbids a person to work without authorization but delays the authorization itself; when it charges hundreds of dollars for the very document required for lawful work; when fee waivers turn poverty into another bureaucratic exam; when basic survival bridges are removed; when the nonprofit safety net has no real capacity; and when enforcement later punishes people for violations that this structure predictably helped create, that is not law and order.

That is bad state engineering.

A policy may look tough on page one. Toughness, by itself, is not effectiveness. Sometimes a system does not prevent illegality; it manufactures it inside the country through delays, fees, procedural traps, and the absence of a lawful survival pathway.

Then the taxpayer pays twice: first for the bureaucracy that failed to let a person work lawfully on time, and then again for enforcement, courts, detention, emergency rooms, shelters, school disruption, and the social consequences of that same design failure.

If we really want less illegal work, less fraud, less homelessness, and less public spending on enforcement, the question cannot be only: who should be cut off from benefits?

The question must also be: Did we leave a realistic path for this person to comply with the law?

Strict policy must be intelligent policy

A strong state is not defined by closing every exit. A strong state is defined by its ability to channel human behavior toward outcomes that benefit the country.

If the government wants less unauthorized work, lawful work must be fast, understandable, and practically reachable. If the government wants less fraud, it must reduce incentives to survive through shadow arrangements. If the government wants to protect taxpayer money, it must count not only benefit spending, but also the downstream cost of enforcement, homelessness, emergency medicine, school instability, and family collapse.

Bad policy often looks tough on the first page. On the second page it becomes expensive. On the third page it creates violations. On the fourth page it demands more police, more courts, more detention, more bureaucracy, and more public spending.

That is not conservative efficiency. That is expensive disorder.

A migrant does not arrive from the American economy

One obvious fact often disappears from immigration policy: a humanitarian migrant comes to the United States precisely because the United States is safer, wealthier, and institutionally stronger than the country they left.

That person does not arrive with an American wage. They do not arrive with American savings. They often arrive after war, persecution, destroyed housing, lost business, lost documents, family separation, or direct threats to safety.

For that reason, a government fee of $400, $500, or $560 is not a neutral amount. Inside the United States, it may look like an unpleasant but familiar administrative charge. For a person from a weaker economy, it may equal a full monthly wage. For a family with children, it multiplies across every person and every renewal cycle.

USCIS announced that, effective January 1, 2026, certain immigration-related fees under H.R. 1 / OBBB increase; for example, an initial asylum-applicant EAD, an initial parole EAD, and an initial TPS EAD are listed at $560.1 For a family of five, that one type of document can mathematically become about $2,800 before postage, copies, transportation, legal help, lost work time, or re-filing costs.

In that context, an immigration fee stops being merely the cost of processing a form. It becomes a test: can this person buy access to legal compliance before they are allowed to earn money in the economy where the fee was priced?

Fees of this size are not pocket change even in America

Yes, the United States has large fees elsewhere. For example, access to federal civil court itself can cost hundreds of dollars: the statutory filing fee for a federal district court is $350, not counting some additional administrative fees.2 That comparison shows that amounts in the $350-$560 range are not trivial household charges. They are serious legal-administrative entry barriers.

But the comparison with court fees also exposes the problem with immigration-document fees. A court fee is usually a one-time filing event, often with an in forma pauperis mechanism for poor litigants. Immigration-document fees for humanitarian families can be recurring, family-wide, connected to survival, and connected to the ability to work lawfully.

If $560 is the price of a document without which a person cannot lawfully work, and that price is charged before the person reliably enters the lawful labor market, the fee is no longer merely cost recovery. It becomes a gatekeeping device.

Even for low-income American households, $400-$560 is not a casual disposable amount. The Bureau of Labor Statistics reported that in 2024 average annual expenditures for the lowest income quintile of consumer units were $35,046.3

A fee waiver does not remove the barrier; it bureaucratizes poverty

The standard answer is: there is a fee waiver. But a fee waiver is not the absence of a barrier. It is another door, with another form, another evidentiary burden, another wait, and another risk of rejection.

The applicant must know the waiver exists, understand the instructions, gather proof of poverty, unstable housing, lack of income, medical expenses, or family burden, submit everything correctly, wait for review, and hope the request is not denied or returned. USCIS describes fee waiver requests as a separate request filed with the underlying form where the applicant is otherwise eligible.4

This turns poverty into an additional administrative exam. The fee waiver does not eliminate the poverty barrier. It bureaucratizes poverty.

A work permit is not an instant paycheck

Even when a person is theoretically eligible for employment authorization, that does not mean they will have a job, wages, and stable housing tomorrow.

For asylum applicants, timing itself is a major problem. USCIS describes the 180-Day Asylum EAD Clock: in general terms, an applicant is not eligible for an asylum-based EAD until the asylum application has been pending for enough time.5

But even after an EAD is approved, the chain is not over. It has only begun: status → EAD filing → fee or fee waiver → USCIS wait → card delivery → job search → interview → onboarding → first paycheck.

Each step can take weeks or months. The labor market is not required to hire a person in five days. An employer is not required to understand TPS, pending asylum, parole, or automatic extensions.

A document can arrive after its useful life has already been consumed

In TPS and similar categories, the problem is compounded by limited document duration. If the government takes too long to process a document, or the document arrives close to expiration, it becomes a strange object: formally an authorization, but practically a late piece of paper.

The issue is not only the size of the fee. The issue is whether the document has a guaranteed useful life. If the government charges for a document but does not guarantee timely issuance, does not extend the practical term of usefulness when delay is agency-caused, and does not provide a bridge during the wait, the risk is shifted entirely onto the family.

Public benefits were a compliance bridge, not free money

Public benefits should not be understood only as a gift or as easy money. In many cases, they function as a compliance bridge inside the broader state machine.

When one part of the government delays work authorization, another part of the public system historically absorbs part of the survival pressure through food assistance, cash assistance, school support, medical support, or housing-related referrals. That is not merely help to the applicant. It is a mechanism that keeps people inside lawful behavior.

Without such a bridge, the system pressures people toward unauthorized work, homelessness and family breakdown, disappearance from the visible system, missed notices, lost procedures, and further sanctions.

If the government believes benefits create dependency or cost too much, it has many more precise tools: reduced assistance, temporary bridge support until the EAD or first paycheck, child-centered food assistance, automatic fee relief, faster EAD processing, low humanitarian filing fees, emergency-only support, or a short transition period.

A categorical exclusion does something different. It does not reduce the bridge. It removes the bridge entirely.

Policy also sends a social signal

Law is not only an instruction to agencies. Law is also a signal to society.

When the government says that certain families already inside a lawful or humanitarian process no longer have access to basic bridge programs, it sends a message not only to caseworkers. It sends a message to employers, landlords, churches, neighbors, donors, and local communities.

The message is: these people are outside ordinary public solidarity. Even when this is not said directly, the effect is understandable. A private person may think: if the government itself cut them off, helping them must be risky, pointless, politically toxic, or simply not my problem.

Status categories are crude proxies, not proof of contribution

Legislators often use formal status as a proxy: citizen, lawful permanent resident, green card holder, refugee, asylee, TPS holder, parolee, asylum applicant. At a certain level, this is understandable. Government must draw lines.

But formal status does not always measure real economic integration. A green card can be obtained through different pathways. The card itself does not prove savings, tax history, English access, stable housing, or labor-market security.

The reverse is also possible. A TPS holder or asylum applicant may have lived in the United States for years, paid taxes when work-authorized, raised children in American schools, followed procedures, filed documents, and waited for decisions, while still falling outside benefit eligibility because the statute treats formal category as the controlling proxy.

The system can create the violations it later punishes

If a person cannot work without an EAD, but the EAD is delayed; if the EAD costs hundreds of dollars, but the fee waiver delays or complicates the process; if benefits are closed; if housing help is practically unavailable; if children must eat every day; if charities have sympathy but no capacity; and if notices arrive through fragile channels, then violation becomes not an ideological choice but a predictable outcome of design.

The government does not need to order illegality in order to manufacture illegal pressure. It only needs to close the lawful exits.

Then the same system may later use the resulting violation as a basis for sanctions, denial, enforcement interest, admissibility problems, a bad moral character narrative, or future immigration barriers.

Taxpayers pay for bad design

A serious conservative policy must ask not only whether a program can be cut. It must ask whether the cut actually reduces total cost.

If cutting a food bridge pushes families into homelessness, emergency rooms, informal labor, school instability, shelter systems, court systems, and enforcement pipelines, the taxpayer may not save money. The taxpayer may simply pay through a different department.

Budget discipline is not the same thing as moving costs from one ledger to another.

Smarter strictness: make compliance fast, cheap, and useful

A smarter conservative policy would not need to choose between open borders and bureaucratic cruelty. It could be stricter and more functional at the same time.

  • low-cost or no-cost humanitarian EADs;
  • automatic fee relief for families without income;
  • expedited EAD processing for households with minor children;
  • automatic extension of useful document validity when delay is agency-caused;
  • temporary food bridge until EAD approval or first paycheck;
  • child-centered subsistence protection;
  • reliable notice systems for unstable housing;
  • no punitive consequence where the violation was foreseeably caused by government delay and lack of a lawful survival path.

This is not open borders. This is not free benefits for everyone. This is basic state engineering. If the government demands compliance, it must preserve a realistic way to comply.

Conclusion

This problem is not only about benefits. It is not only about immigration fees. It is not only about USCIS delays. It is not only about one state, one agency, or one caseworker.

The problem is cumulative.

The system recognizes humanitarian and protection-seeking pathways; forbids unauthorized work; delays or complicates access to work authorization; charges U.S.-priced fees before the applicant has reliable access to the U.S. labor market; turns fee waivers into a separate bureaucratic process; narrows subsistence benefits; refers families to nonprofits that often have no housing or cash capacity; and then may punish people for the violations this structure predictably pressured them to commit.

That is not merely a policy gap. It is an architecture of forced vulnerability.

Reasonable people can debate what benefits should exist, who should receive them, and for how long. But if the government creates or recognizes a humanitarian status, accepts fees for documents, forbids work without authorization, delays authorization, and removes the basic bridge to survival, it must also accept responsibility for the predictable result.

Otherwise humanitarian protection becomes a paradox: a person is told they may remain, but is not given a lawful way to survive the waiting period.

That does not reduce illegal immigration. It can create a new form of illegality — not at the border, but inside the country, inside bureaucracy, and inside families that tried to follow the lawful path.
