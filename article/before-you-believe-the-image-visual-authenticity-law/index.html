Before You Believe the Image: Why AI-Era Media Needs Temporal and Visual Authenticity Law

Source asciidoc: docs/article/before-you-believe-the-image-visual-authenticity-law.adoc
© 2026 Oleksandr Tishchenko / Marketing America Corp LLC. Distribution is permitted only with reference to the author and the original source.

Abstract

Modern media law still assumes that the dangerous lie is primarily verbal. That assumption is becoming obsolete. A photograph can be perfectly authentic and still be materially misleading because it was taken years before the event it is used to illustrate. A second image can be captured today yet become misleading because generative software changes a face, expression, body, background, object, or circumstance. In both cases, the audience may receive the false proposition before reading a single sentence.

This article proposes a Visual Authenticity and Temporal Disclosure Act: a legal framework built around two independent dimensions of visual truth — temporal authenticity and visual authenticity — together with a platform duty to disclose synthetic or materially altered imagery before exposure. The proposal does not ban archives, journalism, satire, ordinary image processing, denoising, compression, color correction, or non-generative restoration.

The problem is no longer simply “fake images”

The traditional deepfake debate asks whether an image is real or fake. That binary is too crude. A genuine photograph from 2019 can be paired with a 2026 hospitalization story and imply that it depicts the person's present condition. Nothing in the pixels is fabricated, yet the visual proposition can still be false. Conversely, a photograph can be captured today while software changes bruising, expression, clothing, background, objects, or facial detail. The date is authentic while the visual evidence is not.

The first failure is temporal authenticity. The second is visual authenticity. A serious legal framework should recognize both.

Images are evidence before they are captions

A user scrolling a feed normally sees the image before reading provenance metadata or a disclaimer. The image creates the initial belief, emotional reaction, and contextual inference. A label placed beneath an already visible synthetic image may correct an inference later, but it cannot prevent the initial impression.

Disclosure after exposure is not disclosure before belief formation.

For materially AI-generated or AI-altered images of identifiable real people, a covered platform should obscure the visual before first exposure and display a prominent notice such as AI-GENERATED IMAGE, AI-ALTERED IMAGE, DIGITALLY GENERATED IMAGE, or MATERIALLY ALTERED IMAGE. The viewer may reveal it through an affirmative action.

The law is already moving toward provenance and disclosure

Texas

Texas Election Code §255.004 regulates certain deceptive deepfake videos distributed close to an election when the specified intent requirements are met. The rule is narrow, but it demonstrates legislative recognition that synthetic visual representations can function as deceptive conduct.

New York

New York Election Law §14-106 uses a disclosure model for certain political communications containing materially deceptive media. That structure is closer to the proposal here because it regulates the relationship between media and disclosure rather than categorically outlawing synthetic expression.

TAKE IT DOWN Act

The federal TAKE IT DOWN Act became Public Law 119-12 on May 19, 2025. Its subject is much narrower — nonconsensual intimate visual depictions, including covered digital forgeries — but its structure is important: Congress has already imposed operational duties on covered platforms for a defined category of harmful synthetic imagery.

C2PA and Content Credentials

The Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity provides an interoperable provenance framework through C2PA Content Credentials. A legal rule therefore need not invent a provenance ecosystem from zero. It can remain standards-neutral while recognizing signed capture information, editing history, publisher declarations, and interoperable credentials as evidence.

The constitutional warning: Kohls v. Bonta

Any U.S. proposal has to confront the First Amendment directly. In Kohls v. Bonta, a federal district court preliminarily enjoined much of California AB 2839 and emphasized the constitutional sensitivity of political and electoral speech, including compelled-disclosure concerns. The lesson is not that synthetic-media regulation is impossible. The lesson is that the statute should avoid becoming a government truth tribunal.

The strongest target is objectively ascertainable provenance: when the image was captured, whether material content was generated or substituted, whether reliable provenance exists, and whether that provenance was disclosed before exposure.

A current signal: narrowly defined synthetic harms

In September 2026, a federal judge declined xAI's request for a preliminary injunction against a Minnesota law addressing AI-generated fake nude imagery. The litigation does not establish a general rule for all AI-media laws, but it illustrates why tightly defined injury and narrow drafting matter. The proposal here therefore regulates specific deceptive presentation mechanics rather than “AI speech” as a category.

Why the archive problem deserves its own rule

Most AI regulation focuses on generated content, leaving a much older manipulation technique largely untouched: the truthful photograph used in a false temporal context. A photograph from ten years ago can be placed beside a story about a person's present health, arrest, injury, resignation, protest, or disaster and visually imply a current condition that the image cannot establish.

Is this image being presented as evidence of the person's appearance, condition, conduct, presence, or circumstances at a time the image did not actually capture?

If yes, the capture date or archival status should be disclosed clearly enough that a reasonable viewer cannot mistake it for contemporaneous evidence.

Why “ban Photoshop” is the wrong abstraction

A statute should never depend on the brand of software used. Modern cameras and phones already perform computational photography. The legal boundary should be technical processing versus material factual alteration.

Technical processing safe harbor

Ordinary sensor-noise reduction and denoising, compression-artifact reduction, moderate sharpening, exposure and white-balance correction, non-deceptive color correction, resizing, lens correction, dust removal, crop, rotation, and non-generative restoration should not trigger a synthetic-media warning merely because software or machine learning was used.

Material factual alteration

Disclosure should attach when processing creates, removes, substitutes, or materially changes factual visual information capable of affecting a reasonable viewer's understanding — including facial features, expression, age, physical condition, body shape, clothing, gestures, objects, other people, background, location, signs, text, injuries, or other material circumstances.

The key boundary is reconstruction. Denoising a noisy eye is technical processing. Generating a plausible new eye because the source pixels do not contain enough information is reconstruction. Reducing artifacts on a license plate is enhancement; inventing characters that cannot reliably be recovered from the source is fabrication of evidence.

The proposed Visual Authenticity and Temporal Disclosure Act

1. Core rule

A visual representation must not be presented as evidence of an identifiable person's appearance, condition, conduct, presence, or circumstances at a particular time unless it accurately depicts that person at that time, or its different capture date or material alteration is clearly disclosed.

2. Temporal disclosure

When a photograph or video of an identifiable person is used in connection with a current event, statement, condition, office, location, or circumstance and was captured at an earlier materially different time, the publisher shall clearly disclose the capture date when omission would cause a reasonable viewer to understand it as contemporaneous evidence.

ARCHIVE IMAGE — CAPTURED [DATE]

ARCHIVE IMAGE — CAPTURE DATE UNKNOWN

3. False visual contemporaneity

A publisher shall not knowingly present archival media in a manner that materially causes a reasonable viewer to believe it depicts current physical condition, appearance on the date of an event, presence at a location, participation in an event, a reaction to a later event, or conduct that the image predates.

4. Material alteration disclosure

A materially altered representation of an identifiable real person shall carry a clear disclosure when software, artificial intelligence, compositing, or another process materially generates, removes, substitutes, or changes factual visual information.

DIGITALLY ALTERED IMAGE

AI-ALTERED IMAGE

AI-GENERATED IMAGE

5. Pre-exposure platform disclosure

A covered platform displaying a materially AI-generated or materially AI-altered image of an identifiable real person shall obscure the image before first exposure. The underlying image may be shown after an affirmative user action such as VIEW ALTERED IMAGE. A disclosure solely in metadata, alt text, a caption beneath an already visible image, or an expandable panel does not satisfy this requirement.

6. Platform provenance duty

Covered platforms shall implement reasonable mechanisms to ingest, preserve, validate, and display reliable provenance, including C2PA Content Credentials, cryptographically signed capture or editing history, camera-origin attestations, publisher declarations, reliable timestamps, and future interoperable provenance standards. Platforms shall not knowingly strip valid provenance for the purpose of defeating disclosure.

7. Technical-processing safe harbor

Denoising, compression cleanup, exposure correction, white balance, moderate sharpening, resizing, lens correction, dust removal, crop, rotation, and non-generative restoration are not material alterations unless they create, remove, substitute, or materially change factual visual information.

8. Reconstruction boundary

An enhancement becomes a material alteration when it generates or reconstructs visual information that was not reliably present in the source and that information could affect a reasonable viewer's understanding of the person's appearance, expression, condition, conduct, location, objects, surrounding people, or circumstances.

9. Technology-neutral rule

Applicability does not depend on whether the alteration was performed with Photoshop, generative AI, a smartphone application, an automated filter, a neural network, or any other named product. The controlling question is whether factual visual information was materially changed or generated.

10. Archives, journalism, education, and satire

Historical, educational, documentary, journalistic, artistic, and satirical uses remain lawful. Archival media require temporal disclosure only where context would reasonably cause the audience to mistake the image for contemporaneous evidence. Obvious parody and satire should receive explicit protection consistent with the First Amendment, subject to independently unlawful categories of content.

11. Remedies

An identifiable person materially affected by a knowing violation should be able to seek correction of labeling, removal of a materially deceptive version, publication of a correction, recovery of proven reputational or economic injury, and defined statutory damages for knowing or repeated violations. Enforcement should include notice-and-cure protections for good-faith platform mistakes and should avoid strict liability for uncertain automated detection.

12. Standards neutrality and auditability

Regulators should define outcome requirements, not freeze a particular technical standard into statute. Covered platforms should retain auditable records sufficient to explain why provenance was accepted, rejected, or overridden, while respecting privacy and data-minimization requirements.

Risks of the proposal

First Amendment overbreadth. If “deception” becomes an open-ended government judgment about political meaning, the rule becomes constitutionally vulnerable. The statute should therefore focus on capture time, alteration provenance, and material factual reconstruction.

False positives. Automated classifiers can misidentify authentic images as synthetic. Provenance signals should be preferred over opaque detector scores, and users must have a correction mechanism.

Metadata stripping and spoofing. Provenance can be removed or forged. Cryptographic credentials help but cannot prove that the depicted event itself is true. Provenance proves history of an asset, not truth of every proposition associated with it.

Burden on small publishers. Compliance tiers and reasonable implementation periods may be necessary so that obligations aimed at large distribution platforms do not unintentionally make ordinary publication impossible.

Accessibility. A pre-exposure warning must be conveyed visually and through screen-reader-accessible text. The disclosure layer cannot itself become an accessibility barrier.

Government misuse. A provenance statute must not become a mechanism for suppressing criticism, parody, dissent, anonymous speech, or inconvenient authentic evidence.

Why the need is becoming urgent

Generative image systems have collapsed the cost of producing plausible visual evidence. At the same time, image editing is increasingly invisible, automated, and integrated into ordinary devices. The old social heuristic — “a photograph is evidence unless someone proves it was manipulated” — no longer scales.

The answer should not be to distrust every image. That would destroy much of the evidentiary value of photography. The better objective is a provenance layer that lets viewers distinguish four different things: a contemporaneous authentic capture, an archival authentic capture, a technically enhanced capture, and a materially synthetic or reconstructed representation.

The legal principle is therefore not “ban AI images.” It is simpler: when an image is being used as evidence about a real person, the viewer should know what kind of evidence it is before being asked to believe it.

Conclusion

The central problem of AI-era visual media is not merely fabrication. It is loss of context: when was this captured, what was changed, what was generated, and what does the image actually prove? A useful law should restore that context without creating a government ministry of truth.

Temporal authenticity and visual authenticity should become distinct, enforceable concepts. Provenance should travel with media. Material reconstruction should be disclosed. And when synthetic media depicts a real identifiable person, the disclosure should reach the viewer before the image does.
